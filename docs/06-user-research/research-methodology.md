# User Research Methodology

**Status:** Working research protocol

## Goal

Discover real jobs-to-be-done, failure points, motivations, constraints, and trust requirements before choosing the product shape.

## Research populations

Do not treat "Indian parents" or "Indian children" as one segment.

### Age cohorts

- 4–6
- 7–8
- 9–12
- 13–16

### Education context

- CBSE
- ICSE
- State Boards
- English-medium
- regional-language-medium

### Household/device context

- dedicated child device
- shared parent phone
- shared family tablet
- low/unstable connectivity
- always-connected household

### Geography / affordability

- metros
- Tier 2/3 cities
- towns
- rural contexts
- varied household education budgets

## Stakeholders

### Children
Investigate:
- what feels difficult;
- what feels boring;
- what creates confidence;
- what creates embarrassment/frustration;
- how they ask for help;
- when they give up;
- what they explore voluntarily;
- how they use YouTube/AI/games for learning;
- preference for voice, text, video, drawing, examples, stories, physical activity;
- reaction to rewards and streaks;
- willingness to explain concepts back.

### Parents
Investigate:
- homework supervision time;
- tuition/coaching spend;
- why tutoring is purchased;
- screen-time concern;
- safety/privacy concern;
- what "learning progress" means to them;
- what reports they actually understand;
- school/exam priorities;
- language preferences;
- willingness to pay;
- subscription trust;
- desired control over AI;
- what would make them uninstall immediately.

### Teachers
Investigate:
- repetitive work;
- common student misconceptions;
- class heterogeneity;
- homework quality;
- AI/cheating concerns;
- useful vs useless dashboards;
- curriculum/board alignment;
- parent communication burden;
- where technology creates more work instead of less.

## Evidence hierarchy

### Tier 1 — Direct observation/interviews
Highest value for understanding workflow and motivation, though sample sizes will initially be small.

### Tier 2 — Large surveys / official data / academic research
Useful for prevalence, learning outcomes, demographics, access, and policy.

### Tier 3 — Product behavior/reviews/forums
Excellent for discovering failure cases and language users naturally use. Poor for estimating prevalence without corroboration.

### Tier 4 — Founder intuition
Useful for generating hypotheses only. Never treat as validation.

## Interview rule

Avoid leading questions such as:

> "Would you use an AI tutor that personalizes learning?"

Prefer behavioral questions:

> "Tell me what happened the last time your child got stuck on homework."

> "What did you do next?"

> "What have you paid for in the last year to help with learning?"

> "Show me which apps your child actually uses."

> "When did you last cancel or uninstall an education app, and why?"

## Research artifacts to maintain

- interview notes;
- anonymized quotes;
- observed workflows;
- recurring pain points;
- contradictions;
- jobs-to-be-done;
- willingness-to-pay evidence;
- feature requests (kept separate from validated problems);
- segment differences;
- confidence rating for each hypothesis.

## Hypothesis scoring

Each major problem can later be scored on:

- frequency;
- severity;
- current spend/time used to solve it;
- dissatisfaction with existing solutions;
- addressable by software?;
- safety risk;
- regulatory complexity;
- measurable learning outcome?;
- willingness to pay;
- differentiation potential.

## Anti-bias rules

1. Actively search for evidence that disproves the product thesis.
2. Do not interview only highly tech-comfortable parents.
3. Do not confuse parent enthusiasm with child adoption.
4. Do not confuse child enjoyment with learning efficacy.
5. Do not overgeneralize from metro/English-medium households.
6. Do not assume every family wants AI.
7. Treat "personalization" as a claim that must be made measurable.
8. Record what users currently do, not only what they say they would do.
